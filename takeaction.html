🌍

Why we created this page

We created this advocacy page to raise awareness about air pollution and transportation problems in the Philippines. Every day, many Filipinos sit in heavy traffic and are exposed to smoke and harmful emissions from cars, motorcycles, buses, and trucks. According to the Department of Environment and Natural Resources – Environmental Management Bureau (DENR-EMB, 2021), 56% of the country's air pollutants came from mobile sources, including motor vehicles. This shows that transportation plays a major role in air pollution and that we need to take action to improve the quality of the air we breathe. We believe that clean air should be a shared responsibility because it affects our health, our environment, and our everyday lives.

🤝

What we stand for

Our advocacy stands for clean air, sustainable transportation, and a healthier environment for everyone. We want to encourage simple but meaningful changes, such as using public transportation, carpooling, walking, cycling, and keeping vehicles properly maintained. We also aim to engage citizens by sharing educational information, posting awareness messages, and encouraging discussion and participation in environmental activities. This supports the Philippine Clean Air Act of 1999 (Republic Act No. 8749), which recognizes the people's right to breathe clean air and encourages public participation in protecting the environment (Republic Act No. 8749, 1999). Through this advocacy, we hope to inspire everyone to do their part in reducing air pollution and building a cleaner, more sustainable Philippines.

Synthesis & Key Conclusions

What the evidence tells us about EVs and cleaner air.

⚡

1. Local Emissions vs. Power Grid Dependence

  • Battery EVs reduce local air pollution by eliminating tailpipe emissions. Unlike gasoline- or diesel-powered vehicles, they release no exhaust while driving, which helps air quality, especially in busy cities (Recharged, n.d.).
  • Electricity for charging may still come from fossil fuels. Even though EVs do not burn gasoline, the power plants supplying them may still use coal or other fossil fuels (Earth.Org, 2022).
  • Shifting to renewable energy can reduce the impact further. Solar, wind, and other clean sources make EVs more environmentally friendly (International Energy Agency [IEA], 2026a).
🔌

2. Infrastructure Needs

  • Electrified vehicles are a growing share of sales. In July 2026, electrified vehicles (hybrids, plug-in hybrids, and fully electric vehicles) made up 29.5% of vehicle sales in the Philippines (Ogerio, 2026). More people are turning to vehicles that use electric power.
  • Expanding public charging stations is necessary. As more people use EVs, charging facilities must grow too, so drivers can charge conveniently, especially on long trips. The Department of Energy says infrastructure, policies, and safety capabilities need to keep pace with adoption (Department of Energy [DOE], 2026b).
🔋

3. Battery Lifecycle

  • Used EV batteries can become electronic waste if poorly managed. Batteries eventually lose the ability to hold enough charge for vehicle use.
  • Recycling recovers valuable materials. Lithium, nickel, and cobalt can be recovered and reused instead of thrown away (IEA, 2026b).
  • Repurposing can extend a battery's life. Batteries no longer suitable for vehicles may still store electricity from sources such as solar panels (IEA, 2026b).

Courses of Action

Practical steps for three groups. Pick the one that fits you.

🚌 1. What individual commuters can do

🚌

Choose e-jeepneys and other modern PUVs when your route has them. Electrified vehicles made up 29.5% of Philippine vehicle sales in July 2026, showing demand is growing (Manila Standard, 2026). Riding modern units supports the Public Transport Modernization Program (formerly the PUV Modernization Program), which began by consolidating jeepney franchises into cooperatives and corporations (GMA Integrated News, 2024). For the policy and governance side of e-vehicles in public transport, see Napalang and Lopez (2024).

🔋

Dispose of batteries safely. Used lithium-ion batteries are classified as hazardous waste in the Philippines, so keep them out of household trash (Ministry of the Environment, Japan, 2025). Return them to the dealer or an accredited recycler, because the country still lacks a dedicated EV-battery law (Enviliance, 2026) and has limited collection and recycling infrastructure (Ministry of the Environment, Japan, 2025).

📢

Spread awareness. Share the full picture: battery EVs have zero tailpipe emissions, but battery disposal and the power source still matter (Recharged, n.d.). Report smoke-belching vehicles to the LTO with photo evidence; the agency has acted on public reports before (Interaksyon, 2026).

🏢 2. What transport operators and businesses can do

💰

Use EVIDA incentives to upgrade fleets.

  • Battery EV users get a 30% discount on the Motor Vehicle User's Charge, and hybrid users get 15%. The discounts also cover vehicle registration and inspection fees and run for eight years from the law's effectivity, until 2030 (GSMH Law, n.d.).
  • Import duties on covered EVs, including hybrids and plug-in hybrids, are 0% until 2028 under Executive Order (EO) 12 as expanded by EO 62, which are separate from EVIDA (ASEAN Briefing, n.d.; Expressway.ph, n.d.).
  • EVIDA's 5% fleet requirement covers industrial and commercial companies, public transport operators, and government fleets within the Comprehensive Roadmap for the Electric Vehicle Industry (CREVI) timeline, so upgrading early helps with compliance (Philstar, 2022).
🔌

Install EV chargers. The country had 1,876 registered charging stations as of August 2026 (DOE, 2026a), which is still limited. EVIDA exempts imported completely built charging stations from duties for eight years, until 2030 (Republic Act No. 11697, 2022). New buildings and establishments must also set aside EV parking slots, at least 5% where there are 20 or more slots (Conventus Law, n.d.), and charging stations are to be installed in those slots under the CREVI (Republic Act No. 11697, 2022).

♻️

Plan for battery end-of-life. Choose suppliers with take-back or recycling partners. For example, Toyota Motor Philippines has trained a local dismantler, En Tsumugi ELV Dismantler Corporation in Pampanga, in EV battery handling (Tribune, 2026).

🏛️ 3. What government and LGUs must do

✅

Enforce the 5% EV quota. EVIDA requires local government units (LGUs), national agencies, and government-owned and -controlled corporations to keep at least 5% of their fleets electric on the CREVI timeline (Philstar, 2022). The government-fleet target is 5% by 2034, and in 2024 the President directed agencies to target 10% (Presidential Communications Office, 2024). Cebu City has said its future vehicle purchases will follow the quota, and Quezon City procured eight electric buses for its free QCity Bus service (Gulf News, 2024).

☀️

Support clean power. Distribution utilities must provide the power needed to establish and operate charging stations (Republic Act No. 11697, 2022). Because EVs are only as clean as the grid that charges them, LGUs should favor renewable electricity for public chargers and fleets. This is our recommendation rather than a legal requirement.

🅿

Enforce the charging and parking rules. LGUs may not issue permits for the construction or renovation of a building or establishment unless it meets the EV parking requirement (Conventus Law, n.d.).

📦

Close the battery gap. Used lithium-ion batteries are already classified as hazardous waste, but collection and recycling infrastructure is limited (Ministry of the Environment, Japan, 2025). Set up collection points and have the Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) enforce safe disposal.

🤖

Prepare AV rules before AVs arrive. The UK plans full implementation of its Automated Vehicles Act in 2027 (UK Department for Transport, 2024), which gives Philippine regulators a model.
